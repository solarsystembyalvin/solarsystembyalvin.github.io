01 · Overview

What is the Solar System?

The Solar System is made up of:

  • The Sun
  • Eight planets: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune
  • More than 290 moons
  • Millions of asteroids and comets

02 · Age

How old is the Solar System?

The Solar System formed approximately 4.568 billion years ago, or 4.6 billion rounded, with the Sun being the first celestial body inside it.[1]

4.568 billion years agoToday

Everything in this timeline begins with a single cloud collapsing and the Sun igniting.

03 · The beginning

A nebula

A nebula is a large cloud of gas and dust in space. The word comes from the Latin for “cloud”.[3]

About 4.6 billion years ago, a giant nebula existed in a region of the Milky Way. It shrank and spun faster, and most of its material gathered in the hot centre. Temperature and pressure became so great that nuclear fusion began, forming the Sun.[2]

04 · Gravity

Gravity and collapse

Gravity pulled the gas and dust closer together. As more material gathered, the cloud became denser and collapsed under its own gravity. Most of it moved to the centre, creating the Sun, while the rest went on to form the planets and other objects.[1]

05 · The disk

The spinning disk

Diagram of a protoplanetary disk

As the nebula collapsed, it spun faster and faster, causing material to spread out into a flat, disk-shaped structure around the young Sun.

Gas and dust remained in this spinning disk, where particles collided and stuck together to form planets.[4]

06 · The Sun

The birth of the Sun

Material at the centre of the nebula built up because gravity kept pulling more gas and dust inward. As it was squeezed together, pressure and temperature rose, making the centre hotter and denser.

Then the core became hot enough for nuclear fusion to begin: hydrogen atoms fused into helium and released huge amounts of energy. That is how the Sun was born.[2]

Hydrogen → Helium + energy
07 · Accretion

How planets began to form

Dust and small rocks in the spinning disk collided and stuck together. Over time they grew larger and larger, forming rocks, planetesimals and eventually planets. This process is known as accretion.[4]

Dust → pebbles → rocks → planetesimals → planets

08 · Two kinds of planet

Rocky planets and giant planets

Inner planets from Mercury to Mars are small, rocky and dense, while the outer planets (Jupiter to Neptune) are much larger and made mostly of gas and ice. The difference was caused by distance from the young Sun.[1]

Hot inner disk

Close to the young Sun, temperatures were very high, so only rock and metal could stay solid and collect to form planets. Result: small, rocky, dense planets (Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars).

Cold outer disk

Further out it was cooler, which let gases and ice gather as well, so the planets grew much larger. Result: gas and ice giants (Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune).

09 · Leftovers

Leftover objects

After the planets formed, there were still leftover objects: asteroids, comets and dwarf planets.

Pluto, the famous dwarf planet
Asteroids
Small rocky objects that orbit the Sun. Most gather in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter.[5]
Comets
Made of ice, dust and rock. Near the Sun the ice heats up and forms the glowing tail we see.[5]
Dwarf planets
Round objects that orbit the Sun but have not cleared their orbital path of other objects. One of them is the famous Pluto.[6]
10 · Evidence

What scientists learnt

Scientists have studied several kinds of evidence:

  • Meteorites: pieces of rock that fell to Earth. They contain some of the oldest materials in the Solar System and show what it was made of.
  • Asteroids: help scientists understand how planets were built.
  • Comets and disks around young stars like our Sun.
